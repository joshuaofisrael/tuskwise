African forest elephant facts

Last updated 8 October 2026

The African forest elephant (Loxodonta cyclotis) is the smallest and least known of the three elephant species. It lives in the rainforests of Central Africa and parts of West Africa, has rounder ears and straighter, downward pointing tusks than the savanna elephant, and eats a lot of fruit, which makes it a vital seed disperser. The IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered after a decline of more than 86% over 31 years.

At a glance

  • Scientific name: Loxodonta cyclotis
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (assessed 2021)
  • Size: about 2 to 5 tons and 8 to 10 feet (about 2.4 to 3 m) tall, per WWF; the smallest elephant species
  • Range: rainforests of Central Africa, with smaller populations in West Africa
  • Diet: fruit, leaves, seeds, branches and bark (browser and frugivore)
  • Population: about 135,690 in surveyed areas (IUCN 2024 status report)

A separate species

For most of the twentieth century, forest and savanna elephants were treated as two forms of one African elephant. Genetic research built a consensus that they are distinct species, and in March 2021 the IUCN assessed them separately for the first time. That change matters for conservation: the forest elephant is in a much worse position than the savanna elephant, and lumping them together hid it.

How to recognize a forest elephant

Forest elephants are smaller and more compact than savanna elephants. Their ears are more oval or rounded, and their tusks are straighter and point downward rather than curving outward. WWF also notes differences in the size and shape of the skull and skeleton. Like savanna elephants, both sexes usually have tusks and the trunk tip has two finger like projections. Where forest elephants live

Forest elephants live in moist and semi deciduous rainforests of the Congo Basin and in a range of habitats in West Africa. The IUCN reports that Central Africa holds just under 95% of the global population: Gabon alone accounts for about 66% and the Republic of the Congo about 19%, with the rest of the region's elephants spread across four other Central African countries. About 5% live in West Africa, in countries such as Côte d'Ivoire, Liberia and Ghana, and less than 1% in East and Southern Africa. The IUCN's 2021 assessment noted that the species occupies only about a quarter of its historic range.

Because they live under dense canopy, forest elephants are rarely seen in the open. Researchers count them mostly through dung surveys and, increasingly, DNA "fingerprints" taken from dung, which let scientists identify individuals and estimate numbers much more reliably. Forest elephants also gather at natural clearings and mineral licks, known as bais, to drink and take in minerals, and these are among the few places where they can be watched in the open.

Diet and seed dispersal

San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance describes forest elephants as browser frugivores: they eat leaves, fruit, seeds, branches and bark. Because fruit makes up so much of their diet, they swallow huge numbers of seeds and drop them, ready fertilized, far from the parent tree. WWF calls the forest elephant a "mega gardener of the forest" and notes that some rainforest tree seeds only germinate after passing through an elephant's digestive tract.

Forest elephants also shape the forest structure. A 2019 modelling study by Berzaghi and colleagues in Nature Geoscience found that by thinning out small trees, forest elephants favor fewer, larger trees with dense wood, increasing above ground biomass in Central African forests. The authors estimated that losing forest elephants would reduce that biomass by about 7%.

Family life and breeding

Forest elephants live in smaller family groups than savanna elephants; WWF gives a size of up to about 20 individuals. They reproduce more slowly than savanna elephants, so populations recover slowly after losses. That slow breeding is one reason the IUCN stresses that even improved numbers do not mean the species is safe.

Threats and status

The IUCN lists the forest elephant as Critically Endangered, after a decline of more than 86% over the 31 years to 2015, driven mainly by poaching for ivory and by habitat loss. WWF reports that forest elephant populations fell by 62% between 2002 and 2011 alone. WWF notes that logging, mining and plantations not only destroy habitat but also open access to remote elephant forests for poachers.

The 2024 status report brought some cautious good news: better DNA surveys found more elephants than previously known, especially in Gabon, and populations have stabilized in some well managed protected areas of Gabon and the Republic of the Congo. But declines continue elsewhere. The IUCN reports that two key landscapes, the Okapi Wildlife Reserve in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and the W Arly Pendjari complex in West Africa, lost around 7,000 elephants combined. Frequently asked questions

How is a forest elephant different from a savanna elephant?

Forest elephants are smaller, with more oval ears and straighter tusks that point downward, and they differ in skull and skeleton shape (WWF). They live in dense rainforest rather than open country, eat more fruit, live in smaller family groups and breed more slowly.

How many African forest elephants are there?

The IUCN's African Forest Elephant Status Report 2024, released in November 2025, estimated about 135,690 forest elephants in areas surveyed between 2016 and 2022 (95% confidence range 99,343 to 172,297), plus perhaps 7,728 to 10,990 more in areas without systematic surveys. The IUCN says the higher figure reflects better DNA based counting, not population growth.

Which country has the most forest elephants?

Gabon. The IUCN reports that Gabon holds about 66% of the global forest elephant population and the Republic of the Congo about 19%, with Central Africa as a whole holding just under 95%.

Why are forest elephants called gardeners of the forest?

Because fruit makes up much of their diet, they swallow seeds and deposit them far away in dung, and WWF notes that some rainforest tree seeds only germinate after passing through an elephant's gut. A 2019 study also found that forest elephants' browsing favors large, dense trees that store more carbon.
