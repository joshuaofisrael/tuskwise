Are elephants afraid of bees?

Published 28 October 2026 · Last reviewed 28 October 2026 · By the TuskWise team

Yes. Elephants are big enough to shrug off most animals, but they clearly avoid honey bees. In field experiments, wild African elephants walked or ran away within seconds of hearing a recording of a disturbed hive, and wild Asian elephants in Sri Lanka did the same with the sound of Asian honey bees. African elephants even have a distinct warning rumble that seems to mean "bees". Conservationists now use this fear to protect farms with beehive fences.

The short answer

Yes, elephants avoid bees, and the evidence is surprisingly strong. Elephants have almost no predators as adults, yet wild African elephants reliably walk or run away when they hear the angry buzz of a disturbed honey bee colony. Asian elephants react the same way to their own local bees. African elephants go a step further: they appear to have a specific alarm call for bees that sends other elephants away too. That reaction is now a practical conservation tool, because a fence made of beehives can keep elephants out of farmland without hurting them.

The first clues: trees with hives

Field researchers in Kenya noticed that elephants, which strip bark and push over trees across the savanna, tended to leave trees with active beehives alone. In 2002, Fritz Vollrath and Iain Douglas-Hamilton reported that African honey bees deterred elephants from damaging the vegetation and trees that housed their hives, and suggested bees could be used to protect selected trees and even whole areas from elephant damage. That idea set up the experiments that followed.

Elephants run from the sound alone

To test whether elephants recognise bees, Lucy King and colleagues ran a playback experiment in Samburu and Buffalo Springs reserves in northern Kenya in 2007. They recorded a disturbed colony of African honey bees, hid a wireless speaker in a fake tree trunk about 10 metres from elephant families resting in the midday shade, and played either the bee buzz or a control of natural white noise taken from a waterfall recording.

The difference was stark. Of 17 families that heard the bees, 16 (94%) left their resting tree within 80 seconds, and half of those moved within the first 10 seconds. Of 15 families that heard the white noise, none had moved after 10 seconds and only 4 had moved after 80 seconds. Many elephants did not simply stroll off; they walked or ran away, often shaking their heads. No bees were present at all, so the elephants were reacting to the sound alone.

A special rumble that means "bees"

Elephants are highly social and talk to each other with low-frequency rumbles (see how elephants communicate over long distances). In a 2010 follow-up published in PLoS ONE, King and colleagues found that elephants hearing bee sounds not only retreated but also shook their heads and threw dust more often, behaviours that may help knock off or block stinging bees. They also gave a distinctive rumble whose second formant, a resonance shaped by the vocal tract, was shifted upward compared with rumbles made after the white-noise control.

The team then recorded these "bee rumbles" and played them back to other elephant families, with no bee sounds involved. Those elephants shook their heads more and retreated further and faster than they did to an ordinary rumble. The authors suggest the call may work as a referential signal: a warning that tells nearby elephants about a particular threat, in this case bees. It is one more example of how rich elephant communication is, alongside findings on whether elephants have names for each other.

Asian elephants avoid bees too

Asian elephants live with different bees. In a 2018 study in Uda Walawe National Park, Sri Lanka, researchers played the sound of a disturbed colony of cavity-nesting Asian honey bees (Apis cerana indica) to 120 wild elephants in 28 groups resting under trees. The elephants moved significantly further away from their resting site after the bee playbacks than after controls, and they called more, investigated more, and showed more reassurance behaviour. Interestingly, they did not shake their heads or dust-bathe the way African elephants did, which may reflect differences between the two species or between the bees they live alongside. Why would a six-tonne animal fear an insect?

Elephant skin is thick on the back and flanks, but not everywhere. Bees can sting the thin skin around the eyes and behind the ears, and they can get into the moist lining of the trunk and mouth, which are packed with nerves. African honey bees are known for defending their nests in large numbers, so a disturbed colony can mean many stings at once, and young calves are more exposed. For an elephant, moving away from the sound of bees early costs little, while getting caught by a swarm could be painful. The same dust-throwing behaviour elephants use for skin care (see why elephants throw mud and dust) shows up when they react to bees.

Beehive fences: turning fear into protection

Where elephants and farms overlap, a single night of crop raiding can wipe out a family's harvest, and farmers sometimes retaliate. A beehive fence is a line of hives hung from wire between posts, with each hive linked to the next. If an elephant pushes against the wire, the hives swing and the bees come out.

A 2009 pilot in Laikipia, Kenya, used a 90-metre fence of nine linked hives on two sides of a small farm. Compared with a nearby control farm, the fenced farm had fewer raids and higher productivity, and local farmers liked the idea. A larger two-year trial in a Turkana farming community in northern Kenya, published in 2011, put 1,700 metres of beehive fence around parts of 17 farms and compared them with 17 neighbouring farms protected only by thorn-bush barriers. Of 32 successful farm invasions, 31 came through thorn-bush barriers and just one, by a bull, came through a beehive fence. Thirteen elephant groups approached the beehive fences and turned away. The farmers also harvested 106 kg of honey during the trial, giving the fences a second value. Beehive fences are not a cure-all, but they are now one of the best-known gentle tools in elephant conservation.

What about mice?

The cartoon image of an elephant fleeing a mouse is folklore. There is no good scientific evidence that elephants fear mice. Elephants can be startled by any sudden movement or noise near their feet, but bees are the small animal that research shows they genuinely and consistently avoid.

The bottom line

Elephants are afraid of bees in the most practical sense: both African and Asian elephants move away from the sound of disturbed honey bees, and African elephants warn each other with a bee-specific rumble. That behaviour lets farmers protect crops with beehive fences, helping people and elephants share land with less conflict. Frequently asked questions

Are elephants really afraid of bees?

Yes. Playback experiments in Kenya found that 16 of 17 African elephant families left their resting spot within 80 seconds of hearing disturbed bees, compared with 4 of 15 families hearing a white-noise control. Wild Asian elephants in Sri Lanka also moved away from the sound of Asian honey bees.

Why would a huge elephant fear tiny bees?

An elephant's thick skin protects much of its body, but bees can sting the softer, sensitive areas around the eyes, behind the ears, and inside the trunk and mouth. A large swarm of African honey bees can deliver many stings at once, and calves are more vulnerable. Avoiding bees is cheaper than getting stung.

Do elephants warn each other about bees?

Yes. A 2010 study found that African elephants hearing bee sounds give a rumble with a shifted vocal quality. When researchers played that rumble back to other elephants, with no bees or bee sounds present, those elephants shook their heads and retreated further and faster than they did to an ordinary rumble.

What is a beehive fence?

A beehive fence is a line of beehives hung on wire between posts around a farm, each hive linked to the next. When an elephant pushes the wire, the hives swing and the bees come out. In a two-year trial in northern Kenya, elephants got into farms 31 times through thorn-bush barriers but only once through a beehive fence.

Are elephants scared of mice?

There is no good scientific evidence that elephants are afraid of mice. The idea is an old folk tale. Elephants can be startled by any sudden movement or sound near their feet, but bees are the small animal that research shows they genuinely avoid.

Do Asian elephants fear bees too?

Yes. In a 2018 study in Uda Walawe National Park, Sri Lanka, researchers played the sound of disturbed Asian honey bees to 120 wild elephants in 28 groups. The elephants moved significantly further from their resting site than in control trials, and called more often, though they did not show the head-shaking and dusting seen in African elephants.
