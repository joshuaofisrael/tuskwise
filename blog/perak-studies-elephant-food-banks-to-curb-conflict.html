Perak studies elephant food banks to curb crop raids

Published 21 October 2026 · Last reviewed 21 October 2026 · By the TuskWise team

The state government of Perak in Malaysia says it is studying whether to create food banks and buffer zones for wild elephants, planted areas near their natural habitat meant to keep them away from farms. The idea follows repeated incursions into orchards and oil palm smallholdings since late September. No sites have been chosen and no final decision has been made, state officials said.

What happened

On 17 October 2026, Perak's state environment committee chairman Teh Kok Lim told reporters in Ipoh that the state forestry department and the Department of Wildlife and National Parks (Perhilitan) are working together to look for sites for elephant food banks and buffer zones. The aim is to give elephants enough to eat near their natural habitat so they are less likely to move into villages and farms. He said Perak does not yet have a food bank, unlike Terengganu, Pahang, Johor and Kelantan, that elephants may not stay inside designated areas, and that no final decision has been made.

The raids behind the proposal

Farmers at Bukit Bangkong, near Chemor, reported elephants in their orchards on 27 September, 3 October and 11 October, affecting about 9.7 hectares and 12 farmers growing crops such as durian, coconut, jackfruit and banana. On 20 October Perhilitan said it had received six reports of incursions around Sungai Siput, by a herd it estimated at three to eight elephants from the Korbu Forest Reserve, part of a larger group of about 50 animals that has split up. Oil palm smallholders there say about 80 hectares of crops have been damaged since early September. Perhilitan says it is monitoring the herd and will focus on conflict hotspots, early reporting and community awareness.

How food banks work elsewhere in Malaysia

Johor already plants food banks along elephant movement corridors. As Free Malaysia Today describes them, these are not feeding stations but planted plots, with banana and Napier grass, inside designated habitat. The programme began with three sites of more than a hectare each, and the federal government announced plans in July for 21 more in the state.

Why it matters

Asian elephants are endangered, and conflict with farmers is one of the biggest threats to them. Crop raiding is not random. In a classic study in southern India, Raman Sukumar found that crops gave elephants more protein, calcium and sodium than wild grasses and that raids rose with the planting season, which is why fences and scare tactics alone rarely solve it. Our explainer on how much elephants eat and drink covers why elephants need so much food, and our conservation page explains other ways communities reduce conflict. Whether food banks work in Perak will depend on where they go and whether they are paired with protected corridors. We will update this post if the state confirms a plan.

Frequently asked questions

What is an elephant food bank?

In Malaysia the term is used for areas inside or beside elephant habitat that are planted with food elephants naturally eat, such as banana plants and Napier grass, so herds have less reason to move onto farms. Johor already runs food banks along elephant movement corridors.

Has Perak decided to build food banks?

Not yet. State environment committee chairman Teh Kok Lim said on 17 October 2026 that the forestry department and the wildlife department, Perhilitan, are studying suitable sites, and that no final decision has been made.

Why do elephants raid crops?

Crops are concentrated, nutritious food. A long-term study in southern India found cultivated cereals and millet gave elephants more protein, calcium and sodium than wild grasses, and described crop raiding as an extension of the elephant's optimal foraging strategy.
