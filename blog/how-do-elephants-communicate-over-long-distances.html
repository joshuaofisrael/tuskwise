How do elephants communicate over long distances?

Published 12 October 2026 · Last reviewed 12 October 2026 · By the TuskWise team

Mostly with very deep rumbles. Much of the energy in an elephant rumble sits below about 20 hertz, the low end of human hearing, and such low sounds fade slowly as they travel, so other elephants can hear them kilometers away. Experiments with wild African elephants also show they react to calls sent through the ground as vibrations, which suggests they can sense some messages with their feet as well as their ears.

The short answer

Elephants keep in touch over long distances mainly with low-frequency rumbles. Much of a rumble's energy lies at or below about 20 hertz, the bottom edge of human hearing, which is why people standing next to a calling elephant may feel a throb more than hear a sound. Low sounds lose energy slowly as they travel, so these calls can reach other elephants several kilometers away. On top of that, field experiments show elephants respond to calls transmitted through the ground, so the earth itself may work as a second channel.

How scientists discovered elephant infrasound

The story starts in a zoo. In the 1980s, acoustic biologist Katy Payne noticed a faint throbbing in the air while standing near Asian elephants at the Washington Park Zoo in Portland, Oregon, something she compared to the feeling of a deep organ note. With William Langbauer and Elizabeth Thomas she recorded the elephants with equipment able to capture very low frequencies. Their 1986 paper in Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology reported calls with strong components in the infrasonic range, the first published evidence that elephants use infrasound.

Researchers then took the question into the field with African savanna elephants. The work that followed, summarized by atmospheric scientist Michael Garstang in a 2004 review in the Journal of Comparative Physiology A, showed that rumbles are an everyday part of elephant life: females call to find each other, mothers and calves stay in contact, and males in musth and females ready to mate advertise themselves to animals far out of sight.

Why low sounds travel so far

High-pitched sounds are absorbed by the air and scattered by grass and trees quite quickly. Low-pitched sounds have long wavelengths that pass around obstacles and lose much less energy along the way. That makes deep rumbles well suited to savannas and forests where elephant families may spread out over large areas while feeding.

Weather matters too. Garstang's review explains that on calm evenings and nights the ground cools and a layer of cool air forms under warmer air above. This kind of temperature inversion can bend sound back toward the ground and keep it from spreading upward, which greatly increases how far a rumble carries. In the middle of a hot, windy day the same call reaches a much smaller area. Elephants do much of their long-distance calling in the cooler parts of the day, which fits this physics.

How an elephant makes such a deep sound

For years it was not clear how elephants produced their rumbles. One idea was that they work like a cat's purr, with muscles twitching the vocal folds at each beat. A 2012 study in Science led by Christian Herbst settled the question. The team used the larynx of a zoo elephant that had died of natural causes and blew warm, humid air through it in the lab. Without any muscle or nerve activity, the vocal folds vibrated on their own and produced sounds in the infrasonic range that matched real elephant rumbles.

That means elephants use the same basic mechanism people use to talk and sing: air flowing past the vocal folds makes them vibrate. The difference is size. An elephant's vocal folds are very long and heavy, and bigger, heavier folds vibrate more slowly, which produces a lower pitch. You can read more about elephant bodies on our elephant anatomy page.

Listening through the ground

A loud rumble also sends vibrations through the soil. Caitlin O'Connell-Rodwell and colleagues tested whether wild elephants notice these. In Etosha National Park in Namibia, they buried devices that played recorded elephant calls into the ground as vibrations, with no airborne sound for the elephants to hear. In their 2006 study, family groups at a waterhole responded to seismic playbacks of alarm calls: they became more vigilant, froze, and gathered more tightly together.

A follow-up study in 2007, in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, went further. The team played alarm calls from a herd the elephants knew and alarm calls from an unfamiliar herd, plus control signals. The elephants responded significantly to the familiar herd's alarm calls but not to the unfamiliar calls or the controls. In other words, they were not just reacting to any shaking of the ground; they could tell different seismic calls apart. The authors suggest elephants may use the ground as a very sensitive sounding board, possibly picking up vibrations through sensitive receptors in their feet and trunk and through bone conduction.

What the rumbles are used for

  • Staying together. Contact rumbles help scattered family members find each other and keep the group coordinated, which matters for the matriarch-led herds described on our elephant behavior page.
  • Finding mates. Females ready to mate and males in musth make distinctive calls that can draw partners from far away.
  • Warnings. Alarm rumbles alert relatives to danger such as lions or people.
  • Individual identity. Elephants recognize the voices of many companions, and wild African elephants even seem to use name-like calls for specific individuals (see do elephants have names for each other?).

What we still do not know

Exact ranges are hard to pin down, because they change with the call, the ground, the vegetation and the weather, and it is difficult to prove that a distant elephant heard a call rather than happened to move. Seismic communication is also less studied than airborne rumbles: the experiments show elephants can detect and tell apart ground vibrations at relatively close range, but how far real calls travel through soil, and how often elephants rely on them in daily life, are still open questions. Most of the field work has been done with African savanna elephants, so forest elephants and Asian elephants in the wild remain less well understood.

The bottom line

Elephants talk across the landscape in a voice mostly too deep for us to hear, using huge vocal folds, the physics of low sound and the cool calm of evening to carry their rumbles for kilometers. They also seem to feel some calls through the ground. It is one more reason the African savanna elephant and its relatives need large, connected, quiet habitats, a central theme of elephant conservation.

Frequently asked questions

What is infrasound in elephants?

Infrasound is sound below about 20 hertz, too low for most people to hear. Elephant rumbles often have a fundamental frequency in or near this range. Katy Payne and colleagues first reported infrasonic calls in Asian elephants in 1986 after noticing a throbbing in the air near zoo elephants.

How far away can elephants hear each other?

Reviews of the research put the usual range of elephant rumbles at a few kilometers, with longer distances possible in calm conditions such as cool evenings and nights, when the air near the ground carries low sound better. The exact range depends on the call, the terrain and the weather.

Can elephants hear through their feet?

There is good experimental evidence that they can sense calls through the ground. When researchers in Namibia played recorded elephant alarm calls as ground vibrations, wild family groups became more vigilant and bunched together, and they reacted to the alarm calls of familiar herds but not unfamiliar ones.

How do elephants make such low sounds?

A 2012 study in Science showed that an elephant larynx can produce infrasonic sounds just from air flowing through it, the same basic mechanism humans use to speak and sing. The vocal folds are very large, which is what makes the pitch so low.
