Sri Lanka's tusker Kavantissa dies after gunshot wound

Published 18 October 2026 · Updated 20 October 2026 · Last reviewed 20 October 2026 · By the TuskWise team

Kavantissa, a wild tusker described by Sri Lankan wildlife officials as one of the country's largest and tallest elephants, died on the evening of 16 October 2026. He had been treated since late September for a wound to his right front leg believed to have been caused by a gunshot. A court has ordered a post-mortem and a formal investigation, and the cause of death had not been officially confirmed when this was published.

What happened

Kavantissa roamed the Galgamuwa area in Sri Lanka's northwestern dry zone, a region known for conflict between people and elephants. In late September a wildlife enthusiast noticed him limping, and a closer look showed a wound near the knee of his right front leg, reported as a gunshot injury. The Department of Wildlife Conservation began treating him on 24 September. Vets sedated him to clean the wound and gave him antibiotics, and specialist teams later cared for him around the clock.

According to Sri Lankan news reports, the wound showed signs of healing, but in his last days Kavantissa stopped eating and became dehydrated. On the morning of 16 October he was found collapsed in a paddy field in the Siyambalangamuwa wildlife area, about two kilometres from where he had been treated the day before. Wildlife officials said he died at 6:18 p.m. that evening.

The investigation

On 17 October the Galgamuwa Magistrate's Court ordered a post-mortem examination and a formal investigation into the death, with a report to be submitted to the court. Experts including specialists from the University of Peradeniya are expected to take part. The court also ordered that the carcass be buried securely on wildlife department land in a way that allows the tusks to be recovered later, a precaution against ivory theft. We will update this page if official findings are published.

Update, 19 October 2026: The post-mortem examination was carried out on 17 October. The Sunday Times (Sri Lanka) reports that it was led by Professor Kavindra Wijesundera, a veterinary pathologist at the University of Peradeniya, and Hiru News reports that samples were taken to the university's Faculty of Veterinary Medicine for further tests. The court has asked for a reasoned post-mortem report. As of 19 October no official cause of death has been published, so any claims about what killed Kavantissa remain unconfirmed.

Update, 20 October 2026: The official cause of death has still not been published. On 19 October President Anura Kumara Dissanayake appointed an expert committee, chaired by former ministry secretary A. H. Sarath Wijesinghe, to examine the immediate and underlying causes of the death, check whether there were lapses in the tusker's treatment, draft standard procedures for wild elephant welfare and recommend a national elephant conservation programme. It is due to report within 30 days. Separately, police and wildlife officers arrested a suspect in the shooting on 18 October and recovered firearms and ammunition, and News 1st reports that five more suspects have since been arrested and are being questioned. The Criminal Investigation Department has opened its own inquiry and has been told to report by 17 November. No one has been convicted, and we will update this page when the post-mortem findings or committee report are released.

Why one elephant matters

Most of Sri Lanka's elephants are tuskless. Female Asian elephants generally lack tusks, and only a minority of males grow them, so large tuskers are rare and highly valued, both culturally and for the island's wildlife tourism. Before his death, Mongabay reported renewed calls from conservationists for dedicated protection of famous tuskers, similar to the guarding of individual big tuskers in Kenya, while others warned that the model may be hard to copy in Sri Lanka's crowded farming landscapes.

Kavantissa's case also sits within a wider problem. Elephants and farmers in Sri Lanka's dry zone compete for land and crops, and shootings, electric fences and poisoning are among the ways elephants die. Our Asian elephant guide and conservation page explain these pressures. Frequently asked questions

How did Kavantissa die?

Officially, the cause of death has not yet been confirmed. Local reports say his gunshot wound had begun to heal, but he stopped eating, became dehydrated and was found collapsed in a paddy field on 16 October. A court-ordered post-mortem by veterinary experts, including University of Peradeniya specialists, is meant to establish the cause.

Why are tuskers rare in Sri Lanka?

Only a small share of Sri Lanka's male Asian elephants grow tusks, and female Asian elephants generally do not. That makes large tuskers like Kavantissa rare and culturally prized, and it means each loss matters.
