Can elephants recognize themselves in a mirror?

Published 11 October 2026 · Last reviewed 11 October 2026 · By the TuskWise team

Yes, at least some can. In a 2006 study in PNAS, an Asian elephant named Happy at the Bronx Zoo repeatedly touched a white mark on her head that she could only see in a large mirror, the classic sign of mirror self-recognition. Two other elephants in the same study did not pass, so the ability seems to vary between individuals, as it does in chimpanzees.

The short answer

Elephants are one of very few animals with good evidence of mirror self-recognition. In the best known study, one of three Asian elephants passed the mark test, the standard experiment for checking whether an animal understands that the image in a mirror is itself. That puts elephants alongside great apes and bottlenose dolphins, though the result also shows that not every elephant passes.

What the mirror test actually is

The test was introduced by psychologist Gordon Gallup in a 1970 paper in Science. He gave chimpanzees time with a mirror, then, while they were anesthetized, put a dye mark on a part of their face they could not see without it. When they woke and saw their reflection, the chimpanzees touched and inspected the marks on their own faces. Monkeys tested the same way did not. The logic is simple: if an animal uses the mirror to investigate a mark on its own body, it must link the reflection to itself rather than treating it as another animal.

Researchers usually look for a sequence of behaviors. First comes social behavior, treating the reflection as a stranger. Then comes testing, such as unusual movements while watching the mirror. Then self-directed behavior, using the mirror to look at parts of the body normally out of view. The mark test comes last, as the clearest single check.

The Bronx Zoo study

In 2006, Joshua Plotnik, Frans de Waal and Diana Reiss published a study in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences on three adult female Asian elephants at the Bronx Zoo: Happy, Maxine and Patty. Earlier work had used mirrors that were small or kept out of reach, so the team installed a mirror about 2.5 meters square, large and sturdy enough that the elephants could approach it, touch it and even look behind it.

All three elephants went through the classic stages. They checked behind the mirror, made repetitive trunk and body movements while watching their reflection, and used the mirror to examine the inside of their mouths, something an elephant cannot normally see. Then came the mark test. The researchers painted a visible white X on one side of each elephant's head and a matching "sham" mark with no color on the other side, so that the feel of the paint was the same on both sides.

Happy repeatedly touched the visible X with her trunk while in front of the mirror, and paid far less attention to the invisible sham mark. Maxine and Patty did not pass the mark test, even though they showed the earlier self-directed behaviors. The authors argued that this pattern matches chimpanzees, where only a portion of individuals pass, and that it supports the idea of convergent evolution of self-awareness in large-brained, highly social animals. Why an earlier study found nothing

Elephants did not always look so good on this test. In 1989, Daniel Povinelli reported in the Journal of Comparative Psychology that two Asian elephants showed no signs of self-recognition. Interestingly, the same elephants could use a mirror to find hidden food, so they understood how mirrors reflect the world, just not apparently that the elephant in the glass was them. Later researchers, including Plotnik and colleagues, suggested the setup was the problem: the mirror was small relative to an elephant and the animals could not get close enough to explore it properly. The contrast is a useful reminder that a negative result can say as much about the test as about the animal.

How elephants compare with other animals

Mirror self-recognition has been reported in only a small group of species. Beyond chimpanzees and other great apes, the strongest early case was bottlenose dolphins, described by Diana Reiss and Lori Marino in PNAS in 2001. Dolphins that had been marked spent more time at the mirror and twisted to view the marked area. Dolphins and elephants are not closely related to apes, so their success suggests that similar mental abilities can evolve separately in animals with large brains and complex social lives.

That fits other evidence on elephant intelligence: elephants keep track of many relatives, remember individuals for years (see do elephants really never forget?), and wild African elephants even seem to address each other with name-like calls (see do elephants have names for each other?).

What we still do not know

  • Small numbers. The positive result rests on very few elephants. A single success is strong evidence that the ability exists, but not a measure of how common it is.
  • African elephants. No peer reviewed study has shown mirror self-recognition in African savanna or forest elephants, so we cannot assume they pass or fail.
  • Sight versus smell. Elephants rely heavily on smell, touch and sound. A vision-based test may underestimate what they know about themselves.
  • What passing means. Scientists still debate whether the mark test shows a full sense of self or a narrower ability to link a body to its reflection.

The bottom line

Yes, elephants can recognize themselves in a mirror, at least some Asian elephants under the right conditions. Happy's response to the mark test is one of the clearest pieces of evidence that elephants have a sense of self that few animals share. It is also another reason to treat elephants as individuals with inner lives, which is central to the case for ethical elephant viewing and conservation.

Frequently asked questions

Which elephant passed the mirror test?

Happy, an Asian elephant at the Bronx Zoo in New York, passed the mark test in the study by Plotnik, de Waal and Reiss published in 2006. She repeatedly touched a visible white X painted on her head while looking in the mirror, but not an invisible control mark on the other side.

Have African elephants passed the mirror test?

No published peer reviewed study has shown mirror self-recognition in African elephants. The positive result so far comes from Asian elephants, and the question remains open for African savanna and forest elephants.

Why did elephants fail the mirror test in 1989?

A 1989 study by Daniel Povinelli found no self-recognition in two Asian elephants. The mirror was relatively small and placed where the elephants could not get close to it or explore it, which later researchers argued made the test hard for an animal of elephant size.

What other animals pass the mirror test?

Great apes such as chimpanzees, bottlenose dolphins and Asian elephants have passed versions of the mark test. Many animals that are clearly intelligent do not, so failing does not mean an animal lacks a sense of self.
