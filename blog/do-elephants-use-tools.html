Do elephants use tools?

Published 15 October 2026 · Last reviewed 15 October 2026 · By the TuskWise team

Yes. Wild and captive elephants use objects as tools in many ways: branches to swat flies, sticks to scratch skin, and movable platforms to reach food. Some Asian elephants also modify branches into better fly switches, and zoo studies have documented water used as a tool and surprisingly skilled handling of water hoses. The evidence comes from field surveys and controlled experiments, not folklore.

The short answer

Elephants do use tools. Across wild and captive settings they pick up sticks and branches to scratch, swat insects and explore. Some Asian elephants go a step further and reshape branches into better fly switches. In carefully designed zoo experiments, an Asian elephant spontaneously moved a heavy cube under hanging food and stood on it to reach the reward, and other elephants have used water and water hoses as tools. Tool use is part of the broader picture of elephant intelligence, alongside memory, cooperation and self recognition.

What counts as a tool?

Definitions vary slightly between researchers, but the core idea is the same: the animal uses an external object, moved or oriented for a purpose, to change its own position or something in the environment. Rolling a cube under fruit and standing on it qualifies. Scratching your own skin with a stick qualifies. Digging a water hole with a tusk alone is usually treated as body-part use rather than tool use, even though the result looks similar to using a digging stick.

That distinction matters because elephants already have an extraordinary appendage: the trunk. The trunk can grip, smell and feel at the tip, which is why it dominates so much of elephant anatomy and daily behavior. When scientists ask whether elephants are "tool users," they are asking what elephants do with objects beyond the trunk itself.

A classic survey of wild and captive tool use

In 1993, Suzanne Chevalier-Skolnikoff and Jo Liska published a survey in Animal Behaviour of tool use by wild and captive elephants. They documented a wide repertoire: sticks and vegetation used for scratching and fly switching, objects used to explore or reach, and other manipulations with the trunk. Their paper is still a standard reference because it showed that elephant tool use is neither rare nor limited to zoos. Captive animals often show more varied object play, but wild elephants use tools too.

Later reviewers have noted that elephants show unusually high rates and diversity of tool use among non-primate mammals, especially for body care. An elephant with an itchy flank rarely waits for a tree; it finds a stick.

Fly switches and tool modification

The clearest experimental evidence for tool making, not just tool use, comes from Benjamin and Lynette Hart and colleagues. In a 1994 Animal Behaviour paper they described Asian elephants switching flies with branches. In a 2001 follow-up, also in Animal Behaviour, they reported that elephants modified awkward branches, for example by removing side shoots, so the switch worked better. That is tool modification: changing an object to improve its function before using it.

Fly switching is not a party trick. Biting flies are a real cost for large mammals, and a well shaped switch reduces that cost. The Hart studies matter because they show elephants adjusting the tool itself, which is rarer in animals than simply picking up a convenient stick. Insightful problem solving with a cube

A famous 2011 study in PLoS ONE by Preston Foerder, Diana Reiss and colleagues tested whether Asian elephants at the Smithsonian National Zoological Park would solve a hanging-food problem the way chimpanzees once did in classic insight experiments. Food was hung out of trunk reach. Sticks were available. So was a large plastic cube that could be rolled and stood on.

Two adult females never moved the platform under the food. A seven-year-old male named Kandula did. Without long trial and error at the food site, he rolled the cube under the baited branch, stood on it with his front feet, and reached the reward with his trunk. Later he retrieved the cube from different places (including one he could not see on entry), generalized to a large tire, and stacked smaller blocks in unsuccessful attempts to gain height.

The authors argue that earlier failures to show "insight" in elephants may have been design problems. When a task requires holding a stick in the trunk tip to get food, the trunk cannot smell and feel the target properly. Kandula's solution kept the trunk free as a sensory organ and used an object as a platform instead. That is tool use tailored to an elephant body, not a failed attempt to copy a chimpanzee.

Water as a tool, and water hoses

Water can be a tool too. In 2020, Lisa Barrett and Sarah Benson-Amram tested Asian elephants on the floating object task, in which an animal must add water to a tube so a floating reward rises within reach. One elephant solved the task on her own. Elephants that watched a successful conspecific also showed more interest in the apparatus than baseline animals, a form of social learning called stimulus enhancement. The paper is careful: solving the task shows elephants can learn to use water as a tool, but it does not by itself prove which mental steps produced the solution.

In 2024, Lea Urban and colleagues reported in Current Biology on water-hose tool use by Asian elephants at Berlin Zoo. Individuals differed sharply. One female, Mary, grasped the hose behind the tip like a stiff shower head, coordinated trunk and limb movements, and changed her grip to swing the flexible hose onto her back. Another elephant interfered by pulling the hose while Mary showered. The study shows a complex, flexible object with flowing water can become skilled, goal directed tool use, not only random play.

What tool use does not prove

Tool use is impressive, but it is not a single intelligence score. Many animals use tools, and some tasks are easier for a grasping hand or beak than for a trunk. Elephants excel when the problem fits their body: moving a platform, reshaping a branch, spraying water. They may look less clever on tasks that force the trunk to act like a hand while also smelling for food.

It is also easy to overread a single star individual. Kandula's cube solution is strong evidence for that elephant under those conditions, not proof that every elephant invents the same trick. The Hart fly-switch work and the Chevalier-Skolnikoff survey matter because they show patterns across many animals. For social cognition that complements tool skills, see do elephants have names for each other? and can elephants recognize themselves in a mirror?.

Why it matters

Understanding elephant tool use helps keepers design enrichment that matches real cognition, and it reminds visitors that elephants are problem solvers. Animals that invent solutions need space, objects and social partners, themes on our ethical viewing and sanctuary checklist pages. For wild herds, vegetation for fly switches is part of intact habitat and diet.

The bottom line

Do elephants use tools? Yes. Peer reviewed work documents stick and branch use in wild and captive elephants, deliberate modification of fly switches, insightful use of movable platforms to reach food, water used to float a reward, and sophisticated hose handling in a zoo. The trunk makes some classic primate tool tasks a poor fit, which is why platform and water tasks often reveal more than stick reaching tests. Tool use is one more strand in the evidence that elephants are flexible, practical thinkers.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as tool use in elephants?

Researchers usually mean an animal using an object, moved or oriented for a purpose, to change something about itself or the environment. Standing on a cube you rolled under hanging fruit counts. Digging with a tusk alone is often treated as body-part use rather than a separate tool.

Do wild elephants make tools?

Yes, at least in simple ways. Asian elephants have been observed breaking or stripping side branches to turn a large branch into a more effective fly switch. That is tool modification, not just picking up a ready-made stick.

Can elephants use water as a tool?

In controlled tests, at least some Asian elephants have added water to a tube to float a reward within reach, and zoo elephants use trunks and hoses to spray water onto their bodies. Water can function as a tool when it is moved and applied to solve a problem.

Are elephants as good at tool use as chimpanzees?

They are skilled, but the comparison is unfair. Chimpanzees often hold sticks in their hands; elephants hold objects with the trunk tip, which is also a primary sniffing and touching organ. Tasks that force the trunk to grip a stick while seeking food can make elephants look worse than they are.
