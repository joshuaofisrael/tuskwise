The short answer

Around 90,000 muscle bundles, if you count them one by one. In 2023, a team led by L. L. Longren and Michael Brecht published the first complete reconstruction of an elephant trunk's musculature in Current Biology. Working from microCT scans of the trunk of a baby Asian elephant, they traced every muscle fascicle, the small bundle of fibres that is the basic working unit of a muscle, and arrived at roughly 90,000. The trunk tip and its finger alone held about 8,000 of them.

You will often see the figure "40,000 muscles" quoted. That is an older estimate, and the authors noted that the number of trunk muscles had simply been unclear until someone did the painstaking count. Both figures tell the same story: the trunk is driven by what the researchers call the most complex musculature known in animals.

A trunk with no bones

The trunk is the elephant's nose and upper lip fused into one long organ, and it contains no bones along its length. Instead it works as a muscular hydrostat. Muscle tissue is mostly water and cannot be compressed, so when one set of muscles squeezes the trunk thinner, it has to get longer; when another set contracts along its length, it shortens or bends. Your tongue and an octopus arm work the same way.

Longren's team found three main kinds of fascicle. Long longitudinal and oblique bundles run along the trunk and bend or shorten it. Tiny radial bundles run from the centre outward and make the trunk thinner, which lengthens it. A small share of transverse bundles run across it. The mix changes along the trunk. In the tip, radial fascicles make up about 82% of the muscle volume, and the dexterous finger is made entirely of microscopic radial fascicles. The shaft has more dorsal than ventral radial muscle, which the authors suggest helps it stretch upward more easily and curl inward rather than outward.

The design is very different from a human hand. We control a fairly small set of large muscles with many nerve cells each. The elephant has thousands of microscopic muscle bundles, which the researchers think probably outnumber the nerve cells in the facial motor nucleus that controls them. One finger or two: African vs Asian trunks

African elephants have two finger-like lips at the end of the trunk, one on top and one underneath. Asian elephants have one upper finger and a rounded lower bulb. That difference changes how they pick things up. In a 2025 study in Communications Biology, L. Eigen and colleagues reconstructed the trunk tips of three African savanna and three Asian elephants. African trunk tips were about one third longitudinal muscle and two thirds radial and transverse muscle, which suits pinching objects between the two fingers. Asian trunk tips were the other way round, about two thirds longitudinal, which suits wrapping the trunk around an object or clamping it behind the tip. A vacuum cleaner that weighs 100 kg

An adult trunk can weigh over 100 kg, yet elephants mostly eat light, fiddly vegetation. Andrew Schulz, David Hu and colleagues at Georgia Tech filmed African elephants at Zoo Atlanta and showed in 2021 that they can use suction to grab food, a behaviour previously thought to be limited to fish. Ultrasound showed the nostrils widening to increase the trunk's internal volume, and the team estimated elephants could inhale at over 150 metres per second, nearly 30 times the speed of a human sneeze. That was enough to hoover up piles of rutabaga cubes and fragile tortilla chips.

Earlier work by the same group, published in 2018, showed another trick. To pick up a pile of small food, an elephant presses down on it with its trunk, forming a joint that creates a vertical pillar up to 11 cm tall. It pushes harder on smaller pieces, squeezing loose particles into a solid lump it can lift. The study notes that wild African elephants eat around 180 grams of food per minute, which is why speed matters. We cover the daily totals in how much elephants eat and drink.

Wrinkles that help it stretch

The skin matters too. In a 2022 PNAS study, Schulz and colleagues asked African elephants to reach for distant objects and found the trunk does not stretch evenly. The upper side stretched about 15% more than the underside. Tests on skin from an elephant that had died showed why: the folded skin on top is 15% more pliable than the finely wrinkled skin underneath, and at that point the skin takes 13 times more energy to stretch than the same length of muscle. Skin folds protect the upper trunk and give way when the elephant wraps its trunk downward, its most common grip.

Whiskers and "trunkedness"

Elephant trunks are also highly sensitive. A 2023 study led by N. Deiringer found that whiskers are densest at the trunk tip, and that African savanna elephants have more tip whiskers than Asian elephants. Elephants do not sweep their whiskers like a cat; trunk movements decide what they touch. Strikingly, adult elephants showed whiskers worn down more on one side, caused by consistently favouring one side when using the trunk. Like left or right handedness in people, many elephants are left or right trunked. That fine control is part of why elephants are such capable tool users.

The bottom line

An elephant trunk has no bones and is powered by around 90,000 tiny muscle bundles, arranged differently along its length and between species. That lets one organ push over trees, suck up food at high speed, pinch a single grain and sense the world through whiskers.
