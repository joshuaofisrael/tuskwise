African elephants: the 415,000 figure and what came after

The figure you will see most often for Africa, about 415,000 elephants, comes from the IUCN African Elephant Specialist Group's 2016 status report. It covers both African species together, because at the time they were still treated as one. The IUCN's 2021 Red List update still described it as the most recent reliable continental estimate. WWF summarizes the same period as a drop of about 111,000 African elephants in the decade before 2016, a decade the IUCN links to a sharp rise in poaching from 2008 that peaked in 2011.

In 2021 the IUCN assessed the two species separately for the first time. It listed the forest elephant as Critically Endangered, after a decline of more than 86% over 31 years, and the savanna elephant as Endangered, after a decline of at least 60% over 50 years. Those percentages describe long term trends; they are not counts.

The first species specific count arrived in November 2025, when the IUCN released its African Forest Elephant Status Report 2024 at the CITES conference in Samarkand. It estimated about 135,690 forest elephants in areas surveyed between 2016 and 2022, with a 95% confidence range of 99,343 to 172,297, plus a further 7,728 to 10,990 based on less reliable "guesses" in unsurveyed areas. Gabon alone holds about 66% of the species and the Republic of the Congo about 19%.

The 2024 forest elephant figure is about 16% higher than 2016 numbers, but the IUCN was clear that this "should not be interpreted as population growth." It reflects better coverage: 94% of counted forest elephants now come from robust estimates, compared with 53% in 2016, largely thanks to DNA capture recapture surveys such as Gabon's nationwide count.

A matching status report for the savanna elephant had not been published when we last checked in October 2026, so there is no official, current continental number for that species alone. We will update this post when it appears.

Asian elephants: about 50,000, with a big new figure from India

For Asia, the standard reference is the IUCN's 2020 Red List assessment, which collated estimates from all 13 range countries and arrived at about 48,323 to 51,680 wild Asian elephants as of 2018. The assessment is candid about the weaknesses: between about 4,000 and 7,000 of those animals come from "doubtful" estimates based on interviews and expert opinion rather than surveys, and some researchers argue the often repeated 40,000 to 50,000 total was for years little more than a crude guess.

India holds the largest share by far. The IUCN notes that more than 60% of wild Asian elephants live there, and that only four other countries (Malaysia, Thailand, Myanmar and Sri Lanka) have more than 2,000. In October 2025, the Wildlife Institute of India published the country's first DNA based national estimate: about 22,446 elephants, with a 95% confidence range of 18,255 to 26,645, based on genetic identification of individual elephants from dung samples. That is lower than India's 2017 count of 27,312, but the government stressed that the two are not directly comparable because the methods are completely different. The global Red List figure has not yet been revised to reflect it, so a new global total is not yet available.

Why counting elephants is so hard

  • Dense forest. Forest elephants and many Asian elephants live under thick canopy where they cannot be counted from the air. Researchers estimate numbers from dung density, or increasingly from DNA in dung, which identifies individuals.
  • Different methods. Aerial counts, dung surveys, camera traps and DNA studies each have different strengths and errors, so a change in method can look like a change in numbers.
  • Different years. National surveys happen at different times. A "global" figure often stitches together counts many years apart.
  • Uncertainty ranges. Good estimates come with confidence intervals. The forest elephant estimate could plausibly be anywhere between about 99,000 and 172,000.
  • Movement across borders. Many savanna populations cross national borders seasonally, which makes double counting or gaps possible.

So are elephants recovering?

Some are, many are not. The IUCN highlights real successes: savanna elephant numbers have been stable or growing for decades in the Kavango Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area, and forest elephants have stabilized in well managed protected areas of Gabon and the Republic of the Congo. Poaching rates have fallen in many regions since 2018 to 2019. But the same 2025 report describes losses of around 7,000 forest elephants in just two landscapes, the Okapi Wildlife Reserve in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and the W Arly Pendjari complex in West Africa, and habitat loss and conflict continue to squeeze Asian elephants. Because elephants breed slowly, with a 22 month pregnancy and years between calves, losses take decades to recover.

How to quote these numbers correctly

If you are writing about elephants, give the figure, the species, the source and the year: for example, "about 135,690 African forest elephants in surveyed areas (IUCN, 2024 status report)." Avoid adding Africa's 2016 figure to newer forest elephant or Asian figures as if they were one census. And be careful with headlines that say numbers "rose" or "fell" when the method changed.
