What was decided

At a meeting at Sri Lanka's Ministry of Environment on 23 October 2026, chaired by Environment Minister Dr Dhammika Patabendi and attended by the Army Commander, the Director General of Wildlife Conservation, military intelligence and drone units and conservation groups, officials agreed a joint programme between the Department of Wildlife Conservation and the three armed forces. According to reports in The Island and the Sri Lanka Army's own statement, the plan includes:

  • an operations room at Army Headquarters to coordinate wildlife officers, veterinarians and tri-forces personnel;
  • special teams to monitor vulnerable tuskers in high-risk areas and their movement routes;
  • joint raids against elephant killings, the illegal explosive bait known as hakka patas, and other wildlife crime, plus a more reliable system for reporting such crimes;
  • training for soldiers from the Department of Wildlife Conservation on animal behaviour and field handling, with wildlife officers given accommodation and research space in military camps;
  • weekly reviews of operational data and monthly progress meetings.

Around 13 tuskers under watch

The Sunday Times reported that the decision followed the death of the prominent tusker Kavantissa (see our report on Kavantissa) and that round-the-clock monitoring is planned for 13 prominent wild tuskers, including one known as Agbo. Speaking to The Morning on 25 October, army spokesperson Major General Waruna Gamage gave the number as "around 13 tuskers" and said the military's main role would be gathering information on where they are and whether they are safe, using drones and reports from people living nearby. He said the initial stage had already begun. The army's statement cited the recent finding of Agbo in the Tirappane jungle as an example of the forces and wildlife authorities working together.

Why tuskers are so precious in Sri Lanka

Only a small share of male Asian elephants grow full tusks, and in Sri Lanka tuskers are especially rare, which makes each one important both genetically and culturally. Female Asian elephants lack visible tusks altogether. Tusks are enlarged incisor teeth (see how many teeth elephants have), and where tusked animals are killed, tusklessness can become more common over generations, as explained in why some elephants are born without tusks.

What happens next

As of 26 October the operations room was still described as proposed, and no figures had been released on how many personnel will be deployed. A separate expert committee appointed by the President is investigating Kavantissa's death. TuskWise will update this story when the programme's details or first results are published.
