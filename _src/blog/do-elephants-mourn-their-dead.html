The short answer

Elephants do not run funerals, but they do react to dying and dead companions in ways that stand out among mammals. Peer reviewed field work on African and Asian elephants documents animals approaching a body or a set of bones, touching it with the trunk and feet, smelling it carefully, standing over it, and sometimes remaining nearby for a long time. Controlled tests also show that African elephants investigate elephant skulls and ivory more than similar objects from other species. Whether that equals human-style mourning is still debated; what is not debated is that elephants treat their dead as socially important.

What scientists have actually watched

One of the clearest case studies comes from Kenya. In 2006, Iain Douglas-Hamilton and colleagues published a detailed account in Applied Animal Behaviour Science of a matriarch named Eleanor who collapsed after an illness. Other elephants from her own family and from unrelated groups approached her while she was still alive, tried to lift or support her, and later returned to her body after she died. The paper describes trunk touches, attempts to help her stand, and repeated visits over the following days. The authors were careful not to claim that every visit was grief; they reported the behaviors and noted how strongly they focused on a dying and then deceased matriarch.

That kind of attention fits the broader picture of elephant social life on our elephant behavior page: long-lived matriarchs, tight family bonds, and animals that recognize many individuals. When a central female dies, the group loses both a companion and a store of knowledge about water, danger and routes.

Interest in skulls and ivory

Anecdotes about elephants touching bones are old. Karen McComb, Lucy Baker and Cynthia Moss put the idea to a clean test. In a 2005 paper in Biology Letters, they presented wild African elephants in Amboseli with elephant skulls, ivory, wood controls, and skulls of rhinoceros and buffalo. The elephants spent more time investigating elephant skulls and ivory than the other objects. They did not simply investigate every large bone; they preferred remains of their own species.

That finding does not prove elephants know who a skull belonged to. It does show selective attention to elephant remains, which is the kind of cue you would expect if carcasses and bones carry useful social or ecological information. What a modern review concludes

In 2020, Shifra Goldenberg and George Wittemyer reviewed elephant behavior toward the dead in Primates, drawing on published cases and their own field observations. They summarize recurring patterns: investigation of dying and dead elephants, touching with the trunk, presence of multiple social groups at a carcass, occasional covering of the body with vegetation or soil, and visits that can continue after soft tissue is gone. They also stress limits. Sample sizes for detailed cases are often small, observers may be more likely to notice dramatic scenes, and it is hard to separate curiosity, reassurance, information gathering and emotion from a distance.

Their review is useful because it treats death responses as a research topic rather than a folklore claim. The behaviors are real and repeated; the label we put on them is where caution belongs.

Asian elephants do it too

Most famous stories involve African savanna elephants. Nachiketha Sharma and colleagues studied free-ranging Asian elephants in India and reported behavioral responses to dying and dead conspecifics in a 2020 Primates paper. They observed approaches, trunk contact, smelling and vigilance around bodies. That matters for two reasons. First, it shows the pattern is not unique to Amboseli or Samburu. Second, it links death-related behavior to the Asian elephant as well as the African savanna elephant.

Is it mourning?

In everyday language, people call these scenes mourning, and the word is understandable. In science, mourning usually implies an emotional state tied to the loss of a specific relationship. Elephants clearly have strong bonds and long memories (see do elephants really never forget?), so grief is a plausible interpretation for some visits. Other visits may be about checking whether an animal is still alive, learning what killed it, or reacting to novel smells and sounds. Goldenberg and Wittemyer argue that both emotional and informational explanations can fit, and that better data on who visits whom, and for how long, will help sort them out.

What researchers do not support is the idea of elaborate funeral rituals with fixed rules. The documented pattern is repeated interest and care-like contact, not a ceremony with the same steps every time.

Why it matters for conservation

When poaching or conflict kills elephants, survivors lose companions and leadership. Understanding how herds respond to dead group members helps explain social disruption after ivory hunting and other losses, a theme of elephant conservation. It also reminds us that elephants experience death as a social event, not only a biological one. Protecting family groups and older females protects more than population counts; it protects the relationships those death responses reveal.

The bottom line

Do elephants mourn their dead? They investigate dying companions, stay with carcasses, touch bones and ivory, and show a clear preference for remains of their own species. Four peer reviewed lines of evidence, from Amboseli experiments to Kenyan case studies, an Asian elephant field paper, and a 2020 review, agree on that much. Whether every such act is grief in the human sense remains open. The safer, evidence-based answer is that elephants treat death in their species as something that demands attention, contact and time.
