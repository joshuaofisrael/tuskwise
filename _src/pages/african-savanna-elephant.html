At a glance

  • IUCN status: Endangered (assessed 2021)
  • Size: males about 3 to 3.2 m at the shoulder and up to about 6,800 kg; females about 2.4 m and up to about 3,600 kg
  • Range: eastern, central and southern Africa
  • Diet: grasses, sedges, leaves, shrubs, bark, roots and fruit (grazer and browser)
  • Gestation: about 22 months, usually a single calf

How to recognize a savanna elephant

Savanna elephants have the largest ears of any elephant, often said to resemble the outline of the African continent. Both males and females normally grow tusks, which tend to curve outward. The back dips between the shoulders and hips, the skin is deeply wrinkled, and the tip of the trunk has two finger like projections. Compared with the African forest elephant, the savanna elephant is taller, has more triangular ears and tusks that flare outward rather than pointing down. Size and growth

This is the heaviest land animal alive today. San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance gives average shoulder heights of about 2.4 m for adult females and 3 to 3.2 m for adult males, with females weighing up to around 3,600 kg and males up to around 6,800 kg. The largest elephant on record was a male savanna elephant about 3.96 m tall at the shoulder and about 10,886 kg. Unlike most mammals, elephants keep growing for much of their lives, so the biggest bulls tend to be the oldest.

A newborn calf weighs roughly 50 to 120 kg and stands about 1 m tall. Calves gain around 1 to 1.3 kg a day in their first year and usually stop nursing at two to three years old.

Habitat and range

Savanna elephants are habitat generalists. They live in open grassland, woodland and bushland, river flood plains, montane and lowland forest, and even the deserts of Namibia and Mali, as long as they can reach water. The IUCN notes that savanna elephants prefer open country and rarely overlap with forest elephants. The largest single subpopulation is in the Kavango Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area of southern Africa, and Botswana holds the largest national population. What savanna elephants eat

An adult eats roughly 75 to 150 kg of plant food a day, about 4 to 6% of its body weight, and spends around 16 hours a day feeding, according to San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance. Savanna elephants are mixed feeders: they graze on grasses and sedges, especially in the wet season, and browse on leaves, twigs, bark, roots and fruit, especially in the dry season. They drink a lot of water when they can, and dig in dry riverbeds with feet, trunk and tusks to reach water below the surface.

Family life

Females and their young live in tight family groups led by a matriarch, usually the oldest female. Research in Kenya's Amboseli National Park found that families led by older matriarchs were better at telling the calls of familiar and unfamiliar elephants apart and reacting appropriately, which suggests that the matriarch's experience helps the whole group (McComb and colleagues, 2001). Young males leave the family as they reach adolescence and join loose groups of other males or live alone. Adult bulls periodically enter musth, a state of raised testosterone and aggression. Why savanna elephants matter

By pushing over trees, stripping bark and trampling shrubs, savanna elephants help keep open grassland from turning into dense woodland. Their dung spreads seeds, and the water holes they dig are used by many other animals. These effects are why ecologists often call elephants "ecosystem engineers." The same feeding habits can damage trees in fenced reserves where elephant numbers are high, which is one reason elephant management is debated.

Status and threats

Until 2021 the IUCN assessed African elephants as a single species classed as Vulnerable. In its first separate assessment, it listed the savanna elephant as Endangered, finding a decline of at least 60% over the last 50 years, driven by poaching for ivory (which surged after 2008 and peaked in 2011) and by conversion of habitat to farmland and other uses. The picture varies widely: the IUCN stresses that some subpopulations, especially in the Kavango Zambezi area, have been stable or growing for decades.
