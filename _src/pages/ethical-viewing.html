Seeing elephants in the wild

Watching a wild family feed, play and bathe is the best elephant experience there is, and the fees you pay to parks and community conservancies help make living elephants valuable to the people who share land with them. Choose operators with trained guides who keep a respectful distance, limit the number of vehicles around a herd, never chase or crowd animals, and switch off engines when watching. Go in the dry season in many savanna areas, when elephants gather near water and vegetation is thinner.

Choosing a genuine sanctuary

Many venues call themselves sanctuaries, rescue centers or eco camps. The label alone means nothing. A genuine sanctuary puts the elephants' needs first:

  • Visitors watch from a distance, often from a platform or on a guided walk; there is no riding, no tricks, no painting and no tourist bathing.
  • Elephants spend most of their time free to roam, forage and socialize in groups, not chained or confined in small pens.
  • There is no breeding for display and no buying of new elephants for tourism.
  • Visitor numbers are limited, and staff can explain where each elephant came from.
  • Independent accreditation, for example by the Global Federation of Animal Sanctuaries, is a good sign, though not every good sanctuary is accredited.

Red flags

  • Rides of any kind, with or without a saddle. See why riding is a problem.
  • Shows and tricks: painting, football, dancing or headstands.
  • Tourist bathing, washing or hands on feeding sessions scheduled throughout the day.
  • Short chains and bare concrete floors; World Animal Protection found elephants at riding venues were typically chained day and night on chains under 3 m long when not working.
  • Bullhooks (a pole with a sharp hook) carried or used by handlers.
  • Baby elephants on display or available for photos, which suggests breeding or capture for tourism.
  • Very large visitor numbers; the report found several venues receiving more than 1,000 visitors a day.

Staying safe around wild elephants

Wild elephants are generally peaceful but very powerful, and accidents happen when people get too close. Stay in your vehicle unless your guide says otherwise, keep quiet, and give elephants space and a clear path. Never get between a mother and calf. Treat bulls in musth, which may have fluid running from the side of the head and dribble urine, with extra caution. Warning signs include ears spread wide, head shaking, trunk swinging and a step toward you; back away calmly. Learn more on the behavior page.
