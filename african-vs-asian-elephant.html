African vs Asian elephant: how to tell the three species apart

Last updated 8 October 2026

The quickest way to tell African and Asian elephants apart is the ears: African elephants have very large ears, while Asian elephants have smaller, rounded ones. African elephants also have a dipped (swayed) back, two finger like tips on the trunk and tusks in both sexes, while Asian elephants have a domed back, one trunk tip and visible tusks usually only in males. Africa has two species, the larger savanna elephant and the smaller forest elephant, whose tusks are straighter and point down.

Ears: the fastest field mark

African elephants of both species have big ears that cover much of the side of the head and neck. Savanna elephant ears are often compared to the outline of Africa, while forest elephant ears are more oval. Asian elephant ears are noticeably smaller and rounder.

Ears are not just for hearing. They work like radiators: blood flows through a dense network of vessels in the thin ear skin and cools as the elephant flaps them, which helps the animal shed heat. San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance and the Smithsonian's National Zoo both describe this cooling role. For more detail, read why elephants have such big ears.

Tusks: who has them and what shape

Tusks are elongated upper incisor teeth made of dentine (ivory). In both African species, males and females normally grow visible tusks. Savanna elephant tusks tend to curve outward and forward; WWF notes that forest elephant tusks are straighter and point downward.

In Asian elephants, usually only males grow long tusks. Females have short tushes that may barely show, and a large share of males have no tusks at all. The Smithsonian notes that many male Asian elephants are tuskless, and that the poaching of tuskers has affected breeding patterns in southern India.

Tusk presence is not a perfect species test. Tuskless individuals occur in African populations too, especially where heavy poaching removed tusked animals. A 2021 study in Science linked a sharp rise in tuskless females in Mozambique's Gorongosa National Park to intense ivory poaching during the civil war. Use tusks together with ears and back shape.

Trunk tip: two "fingers" or one

The end of an African elephant's trunk has two finger like projections, one on the top edge and one on the bottom, which it uses to pinch small objects. An Asian elephant has a single projection on the top edge and often curls the trunk around an object to grip it. San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance describes the difference this way: African elephants pick things up "as if they have two fingers," Asian elephants with one.

Back, head and skin

Seen side on, an African elephant's back dips between the shoulders and hips, so the highest point is usually the shoulder. An Asian elephant's back is domed or level, and the highest point is often the top of the back or head. Asian elephants also have two rounded domes on top of the head with a dent between them, while African elephants have a single, smoother curve. African elephant skin is typically more deeply wrinkled.

Size: which elephant is largest?

The African savanna elephant is the largest living land animal. San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance gives average shoulder heights of about 2.4 m for females and 3 to 3.2 m for males, with males reaching about 6,800 kg. The largest elephant on record, an adult male savanna elephant, stood about 3.96 m at the shoulder and weighed about 10,886 kg.

Asian elephants are somewhat smaller: the Smithsonian's National Zoo gives an average adult weight of about 2,750 to 5,420 kg. African forest elephants are the smallest of the three; WWF gives a range of 2 to 5 tons and 8 to 10 feet in height. In all three species, males are larger than females, and elephants keep growing for much of their lives.

Range and habitat

African savanna elephants live across eastern, central and southern Africa in a wide range of habitats, including grasslands, woodlands, flood plains, forests and even deserts. African forest elephants live in the rainforests of Central Africa and in parts of West Africa; the IUCN reports that Gabon alone holds about 66% of the species and the Republic of the Congo about 19%. Asian elephants occur in 13 countries: Bangladesh, Bhutan, Cambodia, China, India, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Thailand and Viet Nam. Conservation status and population

All three species are threatened. In 2021 the IUCN listed the African forest elephant as Critically Endangered, after a decline of more than 86% over 31 years, and the African savanna elephant as Endangered, after a decline of at least 60% over 50 years. The Asian elephant is listed as Endangered.

The most recent continental figure for both African species together is about 415,000, from the IUCN's 2016 African Elephant Status Report. The IUCN's 2024 forest elephant status report, released in November 2025, estimated about 135,690 forest elephants in surveyed areas, and stressed that the higher figure reflects better DNA based counting rather than growth. The 2020 Red List assessment of the Asian elephant collated range country figures of about 48,323 to 51,680 wild Asian elephants as of 2018. Frequently asked questions

Which is bigger, the African or the Asian elephant?

The African savanna elephant is the largest. San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance gives adult male African elephants up to about 6,800 kg, while the Smithsonian's National Zoo gives adult Asian elephants an average of about 2,750 to 5,420 kg. The African forest elephant is the smallest of the three; WWF gives it a weight of about 2 to 5 tons.

Do female Asian elephants have tusks?

Usually not visible ones. Most female Asian elephants have small tusks called tushes that barely show beyond the lip, or none at all. Many male Asian elephants are also tuskless; in South Asia a tuskless adult male is called a makhna.

Is the African forest elephant really a separate species?

Yes. Following genetic evidence, the IUCN assessed the African forest elephant (Loxodonta cyclotis) and African savanna elephant (Loxodonta africana) separately for the first time in 2021, listing them as Critically Endangered and Endangered respectively.

How can I tell them apart in a photo?

Check four things: ear size (very large in African elephants), the line of the back (dipped in African, domed in Asian), the top of the head (Asian elephants show two domes) and, if you can see it, the trunk tip (two finger like tips in African elephants, one in Asian).

Do Asian and African elephants live in the same place in the wild?

No. Asian elephants live only in South and Southeast Asia, and both African species live only in sub Saharan Africa. The two African species occasionally meet where forest and savanna habitats touch, but they rarely overlap, according to the IUCN.
